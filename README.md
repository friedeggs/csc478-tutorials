# CSC478 Tutorials

## Tutorial 2 — Probability Theory and Estimation (`2026-09-17-probability.ipynb`)

Companion notebook to Lecture 2. Interactive demos (sliders) of random variables,
joint/marginal/conditional densities, expectation, Bayes' rule, linear regression
with PyTorch, and RANSAC. All figures are also written as vector PDFs to `figures/`.

### Setup (about 2 minutes)

Requires Python 3.10 or newer (`python3 --version`). From this folder:

```bash
python3 -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate          # Windows: .venv\Scripts\activate
pip install -r requirements.txt
jupyter lab 2026-09-17-probability.ipynb
```

Then **Run ▸ Run All Cells**. The notebook is self-contained: no extra files or data downloads.

Notes:

- PyTorch is only used on the CPU. On Linux, install the small CPU build to skip the
  CUDA download: `pip install torch --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu`.
- If you use conda instead of `venv`: `conda create -n csc478 python=3.12 && conda activate csc478`,
  then `pip install -r requirements.txt`.
- Sliders need the Jupyter widget extension, which ships with `ipywidgets>=8` and JupyterLab 4;
  if a slider shows as text, restart JupyterLab after installing.
- To export a static copy: `jupyter nbconvert --to html 2026-09-17-probability.ipynb`
  (`--to webpdf` also works after `pip install "nbconvert[webpdf]"`). Widgets do not survive
  export, so the final "Static figures" section re-renders every demo at its default settings.

## Tutorial 4 — HoG Object Detection (`2026-10-01-hog_demo.py`)

Companion to Lecture 4 (slides 44–48: IoU, average precision, sliding windows, HoG).
An "I spy" game: find every **3** among ~100 rotated, scaled, overlapping MNIST digits,
first by hand while looking only at the histogram of oriented gradients (HoG) under a
magnifier, then with a sliding-window detector you write.

### Run

Same setup as above (`pip install -r requirements.txt`; this tutorial needs only numpy and pygame), then:

```bash
python hog_detector.py
```

```bash
python 2026-10-01-hog_demo.py
```

The first command runs the self-tests of your exercises; the second opens the demo. Leave
the demo running while you code: it reloads `hog_detector.py` every time you save it.

### What to do

All your code goes in `hog_detector.py`. Each exercise is a small function with a docstring
that says exactly what to return; `python hog_detector.py` checks it.

| # | Function | Slides | What |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | `image_gradients` | 48 | gradient magnitude and angle per pixel |
| E2 | `orientation_votes` | 48 | magnitude-weighted votes into orientation bins |
| E3 | `hog_descriptor` | 48 | cell histograms, stacked and normalized |
| E4 | `iou` | 44 | intersection over union of boxes |
| E5 | `nms` | 47 | non-maximum suppression |
| E6 | `detect` | 47, 95 | sliding window over a pyramid of scales and rotations |

1. **Manual** (after E1–E3): the scene starts hidden. Move the magnifier and compare the
   live HoG with the reference HoG of the training 3s; click where you think a 3 is. The mode
   bar counts the 3s left and your misses. Rotate and resize the magnifier (Q / E, mouse
   wheel) and try other HoGs (keys 1–5, B C U O) to cope with rotated digits. Hold W to peek
   under the magnifier; V reveals the whole train scene. The **blind test** scene is never revealed.
2. **Scan** (after E4–E6): Space runs your `detect`. Boxes are green when they hit a 3
   (IoU > 0.5) and red otherwise; the mode bar shows TP / FP / FN, precision, recall and AP.
   Edit `DetectConfig` (scales, angles, stride, thresholds) to experiment.
3. **Train**: Space trains a linear SVM (given, numpy) on windows from the train scene; Scan
   then scores with it (M switches between template and SVM). Compare the two on the blind test.
4. N makes a new random test scene that you *can* reveal, to look at your detector's mistakes.

Answers are released during the tutorial. After `git pull`, any exercise you have not written
uses the released answer automatically (its chip in the demo turns blue); write your own to
replace it. `python 2026-10-01-hog_demo.py --no-answers` ignores released answers.

## Tutorial 5 — The Kalman Filter (`2026-10-08-kalman_demo.py`)

Companion to Lecture 5, tracking part (slides 101–112: hidden state, the Bayes filter's predict /
correct loop, the Kalman filter, constant velocity; slide 124: Mahalanobis gating).
A car drives on a road, but you cannot see it: ten times a second a sensor reports its position
with Gaussian noise. Fill in the Kalman filter, one step at a time, and watch what each step adds.

### Run

Same setup as above (this tutorial needs numpy, pygame and matplotlib), then:

```bash
python 2026-10-08-kalman_demo.py
```

### What to do

Everything happens in the window; there is no code to write. The right pane shows the filter
with blanks. Click a blank to pick a token (matrix cells: click to cycle, right-click to go back).
A step runs as soon as all its blanks are filled, and each step you finish adds something to the
world view. A step turns green and its blanks pop as soon as they are all right; **C** (with the mouse over a step,
or a click on its chip) tells you when they are not right yet. When your program puts `P` in place (within
2 mm and 2°), the two clouds merge into one solid bunny; **R** or moving `P` brings the clouds back.

| Step | What you fill in | Slides | What appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | motion model `A` (constant velocity) | 112 | a 1 s look-ahead path |
| K2 | predict: `μ⁻`, `Σ⁻` | 109, 111 | the red prediction ellipse; it drifts and grows |
| K3 | observation model `H` | 110 | the sensor-noise ellipse `R` around each measurement |
| K4 | innovation `r` and its covariance `S` | 124 | the innovation arrow, the `S` ellipse, the distance `d` |
| K5 | correct: gain `K`, `μ`, `Σ` | 109, 111 | the green posterior; the filter runs |
| K6 | gate (extension, scene 4) | 124 | outliers far outside `S` are ignored |

Keys: **Space** play/pause · **→ / ←** half a step (predict, then correct) when paused · **V** reveal
the true car · **Z** zoom · **1–4** scenes (highway, ring road, tunnel, busy street) · **R** restart ·
**N** new seed · `[ ]` the filter's `σ_R` · `; '` the filter's `q` · `- =` the real sensor noise ·
**L** lock `σ_R` to it · **X** time strip `p_x` / `p_y` · **Esc** quit.

The mode bar shows how good the filter is without revealing the car: the estimate's RMSE against the
raw measurements', how often the truth lies inside the 95 % ellipse (≈ 95 % if `Q` and `R` are right),
and the mean normalised innovation `rᵀS⁻¹r` (NIS, ≈ 2 if `Q` and `R` are right).

Answers are released step by step during the tutorial. After `git pull`, hover a step and press **A**
to fill in its released answer.

## Tutorial 6 — Iterative Closest Point (`2026-10-15-icp_demo.py`)

Companion to Lecture 6, registration part (slides 96–109: the closed-form alignment with known
pairs, then ICP with guessed pairs; slide 138 summarises the algorithm).
Two scans of a bunny: move the magenta one (`P`) onto the blue one (`Q`). Build the algorithm on
the right by dragging blocks, Scratch style, and watch each piece work in the 3D view on the left.

### Run

Same setup as above (this tutorial needs numpy, pygame and matplotlib), then:

```bash
python 2026-10-15-icp_demo.py
```

### What to do

Everything happens in the window; there is no code to write.

1. **Align tab (known pairs).** Drag variable tiles into the empty slots. A tile only fits a slot
   of its shape: point clouds are pills, points are hexagons, 3×3 matrices are squares. A step runs
   as soon as all its slots are filled, and its button switches on.
2. **ICP tab (unknown pairs).** Only the loop itself is given: write how `E` starts, when the loop
   repeats, and the four lines inside it, yourself, with word and symbol magnets (drop a magnet where it belongs in a line; drag it out or right-click it to
   remove it). Under each line the demo says what it will do, or why it cannot run yet. `align`
   means your Align script from tab 1.

| Step | What you fill in | Slides | What it unlocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| I1 | centroids `μ_P`, `μ_Q` and the centred clouds `p′`, `q′` | 100 | the centroid crosses; **Translate** |
| I2 | cross-covariance `W` | 102 | `W` and its singular values |
| I3 | `R` from the SVD of `W` | 102–103 | **Rotate** |
| I4 | translation `t` | 103 | **Align**: one click, from any start |
| I5 | the ICP script: start of `E`, the loop condition, four lines | 107–109 | **Run**, **One pass**, **One line** |

A step turns green and its blanks pop as soon as they are all right; **C** (with the mouse over a step,
or a click on its chip) tells you when they are not right yet. When your program puts `P` in place (within
2 mm and 2°), the two clouds merge into one solid bunny; **R** or moving `P` brings the clouds back.

Viewer: drag to orbit, wheel to zoom, **Shift**+drag turns `P`, right-drag moves `P`.
Keys: **Tab** switch tabs · **T / O / L** translate / rotate / align · **Space** run the loop ·
**→** one pass · **.** one line · **1–4** scenes (known pairs, two scans, far start, two views) ·
**R** reset `P` · **N** new samples · **G** random start turned by `θ₀` (`[ ]` change it) ·
**V** show where `P` really belongs · **Esc** quit.

The mode bar shows `E`, the error ICP can see, next to the true error, which only the demo knows.
The two need not agree.

Answers are released step by step during the tutorial. After `git pull`, hover a step and press **A**
to fill in its released answer.

## Tutorial 7 — Signed Distance Functions (`2026-10-22-sdf_demo.py`)

Companion to Lecture 8, Volumetric Fusion part (slide 173: an SDF and its zero level set;
slides 178–180: depth maps seen as distances; slides 189–191: marching cubes).
Fit a torus to the depth maps of six cameras, build a coffee mug from simple shapes with CSG, fit
the mug to the depth maps of a real one, and turn it back into a mesh with marching cubes.

### Run

Same setup as above (this tutorial needs numpy, pygame and torch), then:

```bash
python sdf_student.py
```

```bash
python 2026-10-22-sdf_demo.py
```

The first command runs the self-tests of T1 and T2; the second opens the demo. Leave the demo
running while you code: it reloads `sdf_student.py` every time you save it.

### What to do

| Tab | What you do | Slides | What you see |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Torus | **T1** `torus_sdf` and **T2** `fit` in `sdf_student.py`; then **F** | 173, 178 | your torus moving, turning and stretching onto the depth points (9 numbers: rotation, translation, 3 scales) |
| 2 CSG | **C1–C3**: union, intersect, subtract from `min` / `max` magnets; **C4**: the mug from a body, a cavity and a handle | 174 | each operation on two spheres, then your mug; **X** cuts it open |
| 3 Mug | place the start (sliders, **Shift**+drag turns, right-drag moves), tick pose / sizes, **F** | 173 | your `fit()` again, on your mug and the depth maps of `assets/mug.obj` |
| 4 Mesh | **G** samples your mug on a grid, **E** runs marching cubes; draw each case it does not know yet | 189–191 | the march, slab by slab; at a missing case, the cell and every other cell that needs it |

Drawing a case (tab 4): the cube on the right shows the cell's corners (red inside, green outside)
and an orange ring on every edge the surface crosses. Press on a ring, drag through a second, release
on a third to make a triangle (or click three rings). Right-click a triangle to delete it. Once every
inside corner is cut off from every outside corner, the case turns green, fills all its symmetric
sign patterns, and the march goes on. **Skip** leaves a case out, so you can see the holes it makes.

A fit succeeds when your surface is within 1 mm of the depth points on average: the points snap
onto it and it turns violet. A finished, watertight mesh is saved as `mug_mesh.obj`.

Viewer: drag to orbit, wheel to zoom, **C** resets the camera.
Keys: **1–4** tabs · **F** fit · **Space** pause a fit or the march · **Esc** stop a fit ·
**R** reset (tabs 1, 3) / clear the mesh (tab 4) · **H** turn the mug half way round · **X** cut-away ·
**G / E** sample / extract · **I** vertices at edge midpoints or interpolated · **A** released answer ·
**Backspace / Delete** remove a magnet or triangle / a whole row or cube.

Answers are released during the tutorial. After `git pull`, T1 and T2 use the released answer while
they are still TODOs (their chips say so); in tabs 2 and 4, hover a row or open the cube and press **A**.
